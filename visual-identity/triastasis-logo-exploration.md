# triastasis — Logo Exploration Notes

## We have a name. Now what does it look like?

Once the name **triastasis** started to feel right, the next problem was almost harder than the naming problem.

What symbol belongs next to it?

The name already carries several ideas:

- three
- position
- state
- space
- dimensionality
- structure
- form

So the first temptation is obvious:

> Make a 3D logo.

A cube.

A wireframe.

An XYZ axis.

A polygon.

A mesh.

But that is also exactly what every 3D tool can do.

So the actual question became:

> How do we make something that feels connected to three-dimensional space without simply drawing "3D"?

That became the visual problem.

---

# First thought: the infinite triangle

The Penrose triangle came up very early.

It immediately has things going for it:

- three sides
- impossible geometry
- spatial ambiguity
- a mathematical character
- an object that appears three-dimensional while being impossible

That is close to the spirit of **triastasis**.

A form that makes you think about space.

Something that almost exists.

Something that the eye tries to resolve.

At first this felt promising.

But then there was the obvious issue:

> The Penrose triangle is everywhere.

It is already a known visual language.

Logos.

Tattoo designs.

Math illustrations.

Optical-illusion graphics.

Architecture imagery.

So even though the symbolism works, a literal impossible triangle would not really belong to triastasis.

It would belong to the Penrose triangle.

That made it useful as a clue, but not as the answer.

The useful part was not the triangle itself.

It was this:

> Can the logo make the viewer perceive more structure than is actually drawn?

That idea stayed.

---

# Maybe XYZ?

The most fundamental language of 3D is:

**X, Y, Z**

Three axes.

Three directions.

One origin.

That is almost too perfect conceptually.

Triastasis already suggests a triad.

XYZ is the triad that defines three-dimensional space.

So we started thinking about an abstraction of the coordinate system.

Not literally:

> X  
> Y  
> Z

And not the standard colored transform gizmo from Blender or a game engine.

More like:

- three directions
- three arms
- one shared origin
- three independent spatial relationships

This led to several visual experiments.

The useful discovery was that the logo did not need to be a literal object.

It could simply encode a **relationship between three things**.

That relationship might be enough.

---

# Three points

From XYZ, the idea became even simpler.

What if there were only:

**three points?**

Three dots can represent:

- three coordinates
- three vertices
- three states
- three positions
- three dimensions
- three samples in space

There is something attractive about how little information that requires.

Three dots alone are almost nothing.

But three dots placed carefully make the brain start connecting them.

A triangle appears even if no triangle is drawn.

Then another thought:

> What if the three dots were connected by an almost invisible surface?

Not a dense mesh.

Not a wireframe object.

Just enough lines that you start seeing a surface stretched between the points.

Something very fine.

Very sparse.

Something that is almost disappearing.

That was one of the first visual directions that felt less generic.

But it also raised another problem.

A subtle mesh looks beautiful large.

A logo has to survive at 16 pixels.

At favicon size, the interesting part disappears.

So the concept was good, but it risked becoming an illustration instead of a mark.

---

# Three shapes approaching one another

The next idea tried to make the "three states" concept much more explicit.

Imagine three square-like forms:

- one from the top
- one from the left
- one from the right

They are separate.

They are unquestionably squares.

Then they begin moving toward one another.

As they approach the center, they start losing certainty.

The clean square edge becomes less rigid.

The geometry becomes thinner.

The form begins to dissolve.

Eventually the meeting region becomes a fine sparse mesh.

Not a dense technical wireframe.

Something delicate.

Something that becomes thinner and thinner as the structures mix.

The concept was strong:

> Three clear forms enter.  
> Their identities become ambiguous.  
> A shared spatial structure emerges.

It had a lot of meaning.

Three states.

Three dimensions.

Three inputs.

Convergence.

Refinement.

Generation.

Geometry becoming more complex.

And importantly, it visually told a story.

---

# But it did not work

The generated results exposed the problem.

Instead of feeling precise and geometric, the squares began looking organic.

The transitions looked like fabric.

Or membranes.

Or stretched skin.

The mesh became the dominant visual idea.

Instead of reading:

> three distinct things becoming a spatial structure

it started reading:

> three blobs connected by a web

That was not the identity we wanted.

And the more we tried to explain the transition, the less logo-like it became.

This was an important realization:

> A logo should not have to perform the whole product story.

The three-square idea was doing too much.

It was becoming a diagram.

So we dropped it.

---

# What if the mark does not describe 3D at all?

This was the point where the exploration became more open.

Maybe the symbol did not need to show:

- meshes
- polygons
- coordinates
- planes
- dimensionality

Maybe it could instead encode the most fundamental thing in the name:

**three**

That is the part people can remember.

Triastasis.

Tri.

Three.

So the question changed again:

> What symbols can emerge from the number three itself?

Not a triangle.

Not three circles.

Not three cubes.

Something more specific.

---

# Then came the 3s

A very different idea appeared:

> What if the mark is literally made from three numeral 3s?

Not three 3s written next to each other.

Not:

**333**

Instead:

> three 3s arranged so that they form a new symbol.

This immediately had something the earlier concepts were missing.

An internal rule.

The logo could be explained in one sentence:

> It is three 3s forming one mark.

That is strong.

Because the final shape can become abstract, while the construction still has meaning.

The viewer does not need to see the three 3s immediately.

They can discover them afterward.

That creates two levels:

At first:

> interesting abstract symbol

Then:

> wait, those are three 3s

That is much more memorable.

---

# The first arrangement

The first sketch stacked the three numeral forms vertically.

Each was colored differently so the construction could be understood.

The colors were not the brand.

They were explanation.

One green.

One blue.

One red.

Three separate 3s.

They interlocked into something that did not quite read as typography anymore.

And that was the interesting part.

It existed somewhere between:

- a numeral
- a glyph
- a symbol
- a chain
- a structure

The symbol began having its own identity.

But vertically, there was still a problem.

It felt a little too much like:

> three characters stacked together.

The construction was visible before the symbol.

We wanted the opposite.

The mark first.

The trick second.

---

# Rearranging the 3s

Then the pieces moved.

The three 3s were arranged more horizontally.

Suddenly the whole thing became much more fluid.

And unexpectedly:

> it looked a little like a snake.

That was not planned.

But it was hard not to see.

The forms created a continuous winding rhythm.

There was almost a head and a tail.

A serpentine motion.

And then another strange connection appeared.

The word itself:

**triastasis**

has a lot of sibilance.

**tria-sta-sis**

S.

ST.

SIS.

The word has a whispered quality.

Almost like an incantation.

Almost like some invented language.

And yes:

> slightly like Parseltongue.

That was probably a completely accidental association.

But it revealed something useful.

Triastasis does not sound sterile.

It has personality.

It sounds mathematical, but also a little arcane.

The symbol was developing the same quality.

Geometric.

But alive.

---

# Do we want a snake logo?

No.

That would be too literal.

No eyes.

No head.

No tongue.

No scales.

No explicit serpent.

The useful part is not "snake."

The useful part is:

> serpentine motion.

Continuity.

Flow.

A form that passes through itself.

A sequence of repeated curves.

The logo can have that movement without depicting an animal.

That distinction matters.

---

# Then we tilted it

This was probably the most important change after discovering the three-3 construction.

The mark was rotated slightly.

Not dramatically.

Just enough to put it on a diagonal.

And suddenly several problems disappeared.

When the mark was vertical:

> it looked stacked.

When it was horizontal:

> it looked more snake-like.

But on a diagonal:

> it looked like it was moving.

That changed the whole character.

The viewer no longer reads the three pieces from top to bottom.

The eye travels through them.

The symbol gets direction.

Momentum.

Progression.

The three 3s start behaving like one form.

That is exactly what we wanted.

---

# Motion

The diagonal adds something that was not originally part of the concept:

**motion**

That is interesting because the name contains **stasis**.

A word associated with state, standing, position.

And yet the symbol can imply movement.

That creates a useful tension:

> stasis in the name  
> motion in the mark

A 3D model is static geometry.

But generating it is a process.

Manipulating it is movement.

Rotating it is movement.

Transforming it is movement.

So the logo can represent not only a spatial state, but movement between states.

That feels appropriate.

---

# Three repeated forms, one identity

The deeper idea of the symbol is becoming clearer.

The 3s are not just there because "tri" means three.

There is another layer.

The same basic form is repeated three times.

Each instance occupies a different position.

Together they form something new.

That is almost a visual expression of the name itself:

> one form  
> three positions  
> one resulting structure

That might be the cleanest conceptual explanation of the mark.

Not:

> It is a snake.

Not:

> It is a 3D mesh.

Not even:

> It is three 3s.

But:

> A single form repeated across three positions creates a new whole.

That is very close to the underlying idea of **triastasis**.

---

# The importance of ambiguity

The logo should not make the 3s too obvious.

If somebody immediately sees:

**3 + 3 + 3**

the mark risks becoming gimmicky.

The better version is probably one where:

1. the silhouette works as a symbol
2. the viewer notices the repetition
3. eventually they realize the repeated element is the number 3

That gives the logo a small discovery moment.

The symbol earns meaning over time.

That is much more interesting than an icon that explains itself instantly.

---

# Color

The initial versions used three colors:

- green
- blue
- red

That was useful during construction because it answered:

> Where does each 3 begin and end?

But that is not what the final identity should depend on.

The symbol should work in:

**one color.**

If it does not, the geometry is not strong enough yet.

Three colors also make the concept too obvious.

The eye starts separating the mark into three components.

But the whole point of the final design is that the three components become one symbol.

So the color system should reinforce unity.

---

# One-color direction

The strongest first test is:

**deep indigo**

Why indigo?

Because triastasis sits between several territories:

- technical
- spatial
- mathematical
- generative
- slightly mysterious

Pure blue can feel too corporate.

Bright purple can feel too stereotypically "AI."

Indigo sits between them.

It has some of the precision of blue and some of the strangeness of violet.

A dark saturated indigo could become the primary brand color.

For example, conceptually:

> deep indigo symbol  
> warm off-white background

or:

> pale symbol  
> near-black / indigo-black interface

The exact values can come later.

The direction matters more than the hex code.

---

# Two-color direction

If the symbol uses two colors, they should not mean:

> 3 number one  
> 3 number two  
> 3 number three

That would bring us back to the explanatory prototype.

Instead, the second color should communicate:

- overlap
- transition
- depth
- active state
- generation

The strongest pairing currently seems to be:

**indigo + cyan**

Indigo carries the main identity.

Cyan can appear sparingly.

Maybe:

- only where forms pass through one another
- on one end of the structure
- as a small transition
- as a hover or active state in the UI

That preserves unity while giving the mark a dimensional quality.

---

# Other color territories

## Deep teal

A darker teal is another strong option.

It feels:

- technical
- modern
- spatial
- less common than AI purple

It could also connect nicely with 3D viewport and CAD-like visual language without becoming a literal engineering brand.

## Near-black + accent violet

A more restrained identity.

Most of the mark is neutral.

One controlled accent introduces personality.

This could work especially well if **diethos** remains the broader parent identity and triastasis should look like a focused technical tool.

## Monochrome

This should always remain valid.

Black on white.

White on black.

If the logo only works with gradients or overlapping colors, it is not done.

---

# What should color communicate?

Not "three."

The geometry already does that.

Color should communicate the personality of the product:

> spatial  
> technical  
> strange  
> precise  
> generative  
> modern

That is why a restrained palette makes more sense than three categorical colors.

---

# Where the logo currently stands

The strongest current direction is:

> a diagonal abstract glyph built from three repeated 3-forms

The construction is simple.

The resulting shape is not.

That is a good balance.

It carries:

- three
- repetition
- position
- motion
- continuity
- transformation
- spatial rhythm

And importantly, it does not require:

- a cube
- a mesh
- a polygon
- an XYZ label
- a generic 3D icon

It belongs more specifically to **triastasis**.

---

# What still needs to be solved

The concept is not finished.

There are several important tests left.

## 1. Silhouette

Turn the whole mark black.

Does it still look good?

If not, the overlaps need work.

## 2. Small size

Reduce it to:

- 32 px
- 24 px
- 16 px

Does the structure survive?

Can it work as an app icon or favicon?

## 3. Stroke consistency

The three numeral forms should probably share exactly the same geometry.

Same stroke.

Same curve radius.

Same terminals.

That reinforces the idea of one form existing in three positions.

## 4. Overlap logic

Where one form passes another, what happens?

Do they:

- cut through?
- merge?
- alternate front/back?
- share a segment?

This needs to feel intentional.

## 5. Degree of abstraction

How recognizable should the numeral 3 remain?

This is probably the hardest question.

Too recognizable:

> gimmick

Too abstract:

> the concept disappears

The right answer is somewhere between them.

## 6. Angle

The tilt should feel deliberate.

Not arbitrary.

There may be one angle where the overall silhouette locks into place.

Maybe 20°.

Maybe 30°.

Maybe something based on a geometric construction.

That should be explored systematically rather than by eye alone.

---

# A possible design rule

There is an opportunity to make the logo mathematically disciplined.

For example:

- one master 3-shape
- duplicated exactly three times
- fixed rotational or translational offsets
- all positions derived from the same grid
- consistent overlap rule

That would make the symbol feel generated from a system rather than manually assembled.

That fits the product extremely well.

The logo itself would behave like geometry.

---

# Current interpretation

The mark can be described at several levels.

At the simplest:

> Three 3s.

At the visual level:

> Three repeated curved forms creating one moving glyph.

At the conceptual level:

> One structure expressed in three positions.

At the product level:

> Multiple spatial states resolving into a single form.

And at the brand level:

> triastasis.

That layering is probably the strongest thing this direction has going for it.

---

# Current color recommendation

Start with:

## Primary

**Deep indigo**

A single-color mark first.

Make the symbol work before adding anything else.

## Secondary exploration

**Indigo + cyan**

Use cyan as a controlled secondary signal rather than assigning colors to individual 3s.

## Neutral system

- near-black
- warm white
- soft gray

The symbol should remain strong in all-neutral environments.

---

# The evolution so far

The logo exploration has followed a similar pattern to the naming process.

We started literal.

Then kept removing the obvious answers.

**Infinite triangle**

> Spatial and mathematical.  
> Too established.

↓

**XYZ abstraction**

> Conceptually correct.  
> Risk of generic 3D language.

↓

**Three points and a sparse mesh**

> Minimal and intriguing.  
> Difficult to scale.

↓

**Three squares converging into a mesh**

> Strong story.  
> Too complex and organic in practice.

↓

**Other abstract geometry**

> Interesting forms.  
> Not enough connection to the name.

↓

**Three numeral 3s**

> Simple internal logic.  
> Specific to triastasis.

↓

**Rearranging the 3s**

> The mark starts becoming a glyph rather than typography.

↓

**Serpentine rhythm**

> Unexpected personality and continuity.

↓

**Diagonal orientation**

> Motion.  
> Direction.  
> Better silhouette.  
> Less literal.

↓

**One or two colors**

> Stop explaining the construction.  
> Let the symbol become one identity.

---

# The idea underneath the logo

The most interesting logo direction is no longer:

> How do we draw three-dimensional space?

It is:

> How can one form exist three times, in three positions, and become something new?

That feels much closer to **triastasis**.

And it gives the logo something valuable:

A reason to exist that is specific to the name.

The mark is not simply an icon for 3D software.

It is a small visual system.

A repeated form.

Three positions.

One symbol.

**triastasis.**
