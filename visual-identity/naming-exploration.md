triastasis — Naming Exploration Notes
What are we actually trying to name?

The product started as a frontend for interacting with TRELLIS.2 and managing generated 3D models.

So the obvious naming space is already crowded:

    3D
    
    polygons
    
    meshes
    
    geometry
    
    shape
    
    form
    
    generation
    
    spatial structure
    
    model management

The first question was simple:

    What does a name for this product naturally evoke?

The first answer was Polyloom.

And at first, Polyloom made sense.

Poly immediately suggests polygons, many-sided geometry, and 3D meshes.

Loom suggests weaving things together.

So conceptually:

    polygons + weaving
    meshes + composition
    generated assets + organization

That is actually a good description of the product.

But it also felt familiar.

Too easy.

Too much like a name that several people working in 3D, graphics, or generative tools could arrive at independently.

So the search moved away from:

    What describes the product?

and toward:

    What idea sits underneath the product?

First obvious experiments
PolyMesh

This is almost brutally direct.

Poly + Mesh

It says exactly what it is about.

That is also the problem.

It sounds like:

    a mesh utility
    
    a rendering library
    
    a file format
    
    an open-source package
    
    a generic 3D framework

There is no real identity in it.

It is descriptive, but not memorable.

So:

    PolyMesh is too easy.

Meshloom

This was more interesting.

Mesh gives the 3D connection immediately.

Loom gives the idea of weaving or bringing structures together.

That has more personality than PolyMesh.

It also fits a product that generates, organizes, and manages 3D assets.

But again:

    it still feels like the first idea.

It feels like a compound word made from two obvious concepts.

Useful.

Reasonable.

But not distinctive enough.

So the question changed again.
What if we stop naming the mesh?

A mesh is just the representation.

The deeper concept is space.

A 3D model exists in space.

It has:

    shape
    
    extension
    
    depth
    
    position
    
    volume
    
    structure
    
    dimensionality

So maybe the name should come from one of those ideas instead.

That led naturally into Greek and Latin roots.

Not because the product needs to sound classical.

But because so much technical language already comes from those roots:

    polygon
    
    geometry
    
    morphology
    
    schema
    
    dimension
    
    stereometry

So the thought process became:

    What if we mixed Greek?

Then:

    What if we mixed Latin?

Then:

    What concepts actually mean what we want?

What is 3D in Greek?

The first route was to look for the idea of a solid.

The Greek root stereos refers to something solid, firm, or spatially solid.

That is where words such as stereometry come from.

At first this sounded promising.

A 3D object is a solid.

But then the modern association gets in the way.

Stereo overwhelmingly suggests audio.

So names based directly on stereo would probably communicate the wrong thing first.

That made this route feel technically correct, but brand-wise weak.

So:

    Concept of 3D in Greek?
    Interesting, but not good enough.

We needed a concept that felt less literal and more ownable.
What about shape?

If the object is generated, maybe the most important concept is not "3D."

Maybe it is form.

So we started looking at roots around shape and appearance.
Morphē

Greek morphē means form or shape.

This is the root behind words such as:

    morph
    
    morphology
    
    metamorphosis

This is very relevant to 3D generation.

A prompt becomes a form.

An image becomes a form.

A rough structure becomes a refined object.

Possible naming directions naturally appear:

    MorphMesh
    
    Morphos
    
    Morphia
    
    PolyMorph

But "morph" is already everywhere.

Graphics.

Animation.

Biology.

Games.

AI.

It communicates the right idea, but it does not feel unique enough.
Eidos

Greek eidos relates to form, appearance, visible shape, or kind.

This is more abstract and philosophical.

It has a nice relationship with 3D generation because a generated object is, in a sense, a visible form brought into existence.

Possible ideas:

    Eidos
    
    Eidon
    
    Eidomesh
    
    PolyEidos

This is more distinctive.

But it does not naturally encode three-dimensionality.

It is about form, but not necessarily space.
Soma

Greek soma means body.

This was interesting because a 3D model is not merely an outline.

It occupies space.

It has a body.

And if the product is ever used for characters, creatures, or human forms, the connection gets stronger.

Possible directions:

    Soma
    
    SomaMesh
    
    PolySoma
    
    SomaForge

But it also brings strong medical, biological, and anatomical associations.

Still interesting.

Not quite right.
Schema

Greek schema means form, shape, figure, or configuration.

Conceptually this is excellent.

A 3D model has structure.

A mesh has structure.

The software itself manages structure.

But modern software already owns this word very strongly.

"Schema" immediately suggests databases, APIs, validation, and structured data.

So it would pull the product in the wrong direction.
What about the human shape?

This was another useful branch.

Instead of asking only about geometry:

    What words mean body, figure, likeness, human form?

That opened up concepts such as:

    soma
    
    figura
    
    corpus
    
    eidos
    
    morphē

This helped because it clarified something important.

The product is not just about abstract geometry.

It is about forms that exist in space.

Objects.

Bodies.

Figures.

Representations.

That reinforced the feeling that the final name should be about spatial form, not merely polygons.
What about dimension itself?

This became the most important turn.

Instead of asking:

    What is a 3D object?

we asked:

    What is a dimension?

And then:

    What does it mean for something to extend through space?

That led to the word:
Diastasis

Greek diastasis is associated with ideas such as:

    extension
    
    separation
    
    distance
    
    interval
    
    standing apart
    
    spatial dimension

This immediately felt different.

Not because it literally means "3D."

It does not.

But it points at the underlying concept.

A dimension is an extension.

An object exists because it extends through space.

Points can be separated.

A surface spans distance.

A mesh occupies intervals.

A model has spatial relationships.

This is much closer to the deeper idea of 3D than simply naming a polygon.

So the reaction was basically:

    Ok. That's interesting.

The word has the right texture too.

Technical.

Slightly mathematical.

Classical without sounding ornamental.

And importantly:

    it does not sound like a generic AI startup name.

But Diastasis alone is not enough

There are two problems.

First, diastasis already has a strong medical meaning in modern usage.

Most visibly:

diastasis recti

So using diastasis directly would create a lot of unrelated medical association and search noise.

Second, it is missing the number that matters.

The product exists in:

three dimensions

So the real naming puzzle became:

    How can we mix the idea of three
    with the idea of extension, separation, or dimension?

That is where the name started becoming interesting.
How can "three" be encoded?

The obvious option is:
Tri-

Greek-derived.

Immediately understood.

Three.

Simple.

But maybe too simple.

So we also considered variations.
Tria-

Softer.

More naturally blendable into a longer coined word.

Feels slightly more like a classical construction.
Tris-

Suggests threefold or three times.

Interesting, but harsher.
Treis-

More overtly Greek.

Less intuitive for English speakers.
Trito-

Related to "third."

Not exactly what we want.
Tres-

Latin for three.

Very recognizable.

Especially to Portuguese and Spanish speakers.

But mixing Latin tres with Greek diastasis becomes intentionally hybrid.

That is not necessarily bad.

Brand names do not need to obey classical grammar.

But it should still sound like one word.
Tern-

A Latin-derived idea associated with sets of three.

Conceptually valid.

Not immediately obvious.
Trin-

Threefold.

Familiar through words such as trinity.

But carries many other associations.
Early combinations

Once three and diastasis were on the table, combinations started appearing.
TriDiasti

A compressed construction.

Tri + Diasti

It hints at:

    three
    
    dimension
    
    diastasis

It sounds technical.

But almost too technical.

It feels like a code name or internal tool.

The word seems cut short.

Interesting experiment.

Not final.
TreStasis

This one shifted toward another root.

Stasis itself comes from Greek and can suggest:

    standing
    
    state
    
    position
    
    condition

So:

Tre + Stasis

could suggest:

    three states
    
    three positions
    
    three points of standing

This is a nice conceptual connection to dimensional space.

Three positions.

Three directions.

Three axes.

But it loses some of the "extension" meaning that made diastasis interesting.
TreDiastasis

Very literal internally.

Three + diastasis.

It preserves both ideas.

But it is long.

It sounds assembled.

The seams are visible.

It feels like two words forced together rather than one discovered word.
DiasTresis

This was a more playful attempt.

Could tres be hidden inside something that still echoes diastasis?

Something like:

DiasTRESis

There is a clever idea in there.

The "three" is embedded instead of announced.

But visually it begins to look engineered around the pun.

And "dias" has its own associations in Portuguese and Spanish.

So again:

interesting route, but probably not the destination.
Then: Triastasis

This is where things began to click.

triastasis

It has the sound of a word that could already exist.

But it also feels distinctive.

The construction can be read conceptually as:

tria + stasis

Three + standing / state / position.

And because the whole naming path came from diastasis, it also carries an echo of:

    extension
    
    dimensional separation
    
    spatial interval

So even if it is not a literal classical term for "three-dimensional space," it works as a coined concept.

It can suggest:

    three positions
    
    three states
    
    three directions
    
    three dimensions
    
    three axes defining space

That is much closer to the conceptual identity of the product.

And unlike PolyMesh or Meshloom, it does not sound like the first name someone would generate from a list of 3D keywords.
Why "stasis" is useful

On its own, stasis can mean:

    standing
    
    state
    
    position
    
    condition
    
    something held in place

The modern English sense of "stasis" often means a static or suspended condition.

That could have been a downside.

But in this context it becomes useful.

A 3D object is defined by positions in space.

A vertex has a position.

A point has coordinates.

A model exists through the relationships between many points.

So the idea becomes:

    three positions define three-dimensional thinking

or more abstractly:

    three spatial states create form

Not literal mathematics.

But a coherent brand idea.
The visual identity started emerging from the name

Once the name became triastasis, the logo stopped needing to spell out "3D."

The name itself already suggests a triad.

So the visual exploration naturally moved toward:

    three points
    
    three states
    
    three shapes
    
    three axes
    
    three dimensions
    
    three planes
    
    three things converging

This is more interesting than putting a cube next to the word.
First visual thought: the infinite triangle

The Penrose triangle was an obvious possibility.

It has:

    three sides
    
    impossible geometry
    
    spatial ambiguity
    
    dimensional weirdness
    
    mathematical character

It is visually strong.

But it is also already widely used.

A plain Penrose triangle would risk becoming:

    generic clever geometry logo

rather than:

    triastasis

So it works better as inspiration than as the final symbol.

The interesting part is not the exact infinite triangle.

It is the idea of:

    three structures existing in a spatial relationship that is not immediately obvious.

That is a much better direction.
XYZ abstraction

The next idea was to use the most fundamental 3D concept directly:

X, Y, Z

Three axes.

One origin.

Three independent directions defining space.

This gives a very clean abstraction:

    three arms
    
    three planes
    
    three colors
    
    three directions
    
    one shared center

The logo does not need to show the letters X, Y, and Z.

It can simply encode the relationship.

Three forms emerging from a single origin.

Or three forms converging into one.

This is immediately relevant to 3D without drawing a literal cube.
Three dots

Then there is the simplest possible symbol:

three dots

That is almost absurdly minimal.

But it is also surprisingly rich.

Three dots can mean:

    three points
    
    three positions
    
    three vertices
    
    three states
    
    three dimensions
    
    three samples in space

The interesting version is not simply three circles arranged as a triangle.

The interesting version is:

    three points that appear to be connected by something extremely fine.

A subtle mesh.

A field.

A surface.

Something emerging between them.

That gives the dots a reason to exist.

They are not decoration.

They are anchors.
Three shapes mixing together

This became one of the most promising visual ideas.

Imagine three square-like planes.

Not necessarily perfect squares.

They could be:

    planes
    
    panels
    
    patches
    
    surfaces
    
    grids

Each starts as its own thing.

One might correspond loosely to X.

One to Y.

One to Z.

They approach each other.

Then they begin to overlap.

At the boundaries, the geometry gets finer.

The forms start to dissolve into:

    points
    
    lines
    
    triangles
    
    wireframe
    
    denser mesh

The closer they get to the shared center, the finer the structure becomes.

Eventually, the three separate forms become one continuous spatial object.

That visual idea says a lot without being literal.

It can represent:

    three dimensions
    
    three states
    
    convergence
    
    generation
    
    refinement
    
    mesh creation
    
    separate inputs becoming a unified 3D form

This is much more interesting than a cube icon.
A possible visual progression

The symbol could imply a transformation:
State 1: separation

Three square or plane-like shapes exist independently.

They are clearly three things.
State 2: convergence

They move toward one shared spatial point.

Their edges begin to distort.
State 3: interaction

Where they overlap, the clean surfaces break into a mesh.

Large cells become smaller.

The geometry becomes denser.
State 4: refinement

The mesh becomes finer toward the center.

The three identities become harder to distinguish.
State 5: unification

At the center, there is no longer X, Y, or Z.

There is simply a form.

A single spatial structure created from three directions.

That feels very close to what triastasis should mean visually.
The mesh idea

The mesh should probably not look like a normal 3D wireframe pasted onto a shape.

That would be too obvious.

A more interesting idea is for the mesh itself to communicate transition.

For example:

    coarse near the outside
    
    increasingly fine near the intersection
    
    almost continuous at the center

This gives the visual a sense of refinement.

Like three discrete states becoming one continuous form.

Or three coarse representations resolving into a detailed model.

That also creates a subtle connection to generative 3D.

The symbol can feel like something is being reconstructed.

Not simply displayed.
Color as part of the concept

Three distinct colors can reinforce the three-part idea.

For example:

    one cool blue
    
    one cyan/green
    
    one warmer orange or violet

Each starts clearly separated.

As the forms mix, the colors can interpolate.

At the center, the distinctions become less obvious.

That allows color itself to tell the story:

    three independent states become one shared structure.

The exact palette is secondary.

The important part is that the color is doing semantic work.
Why this direction is stronger than a literal 3D logo

A literal cube says:

    3D software.

A literal mesh says:

    3D software.

A literal polygon says:

    3D software.

But three forms converging into an increasingly fine mesh says something more specific:

    separate spatial dimensions becoming a unified form.

That connects directly to the name.

And it creates a logo that can have its own internal logic.

Someone does not need to understand the Greek origin to appreciate it.

But if they do know the story, the symbol becomes richer.

The idea underneath all of it

The most compelling interpretation of triastasis is probably not:

    a clever way to say 3D.

It is:

    three independent spatial states coming together to create form.

That can describe:

    X, Y, Z
    
    three positions
    
    three planes
    
    three points
    
    three dimensions
    
    three representations
    
    three states of refinement

And visually, it gives us a very strong motif:

    three things approach each other, interact, and become a finer unified mesh.

That feels specific enough to build an identity around.

It also leaves room for the product to grow beyond TRELLIS.2.

The name is not tied to one model.

The symbol is not tied to one generation method.

The underlying idea remains:

form emerging from three-dimensional structure.
